In [EP8](/ep/8/) the RMM vendor's own hosted platform went offline to get ahead of a disclosure. This week the RMM was the attack path, and the FBI published a FLASH advisory on an AI framework that runs the same techniques we have been defending against for twenty years.

## ARTEX: the FBI's advisory, and the reality check

**Sourcing:** FBI FLASH advisory FLASH-20261113-001, released November 13 through IC3 and marked TLP:CLEAR, as the primary document. Jeffrey Appel's analysis for the technique-age table and the Defender mapping. BleepingComputer and The Record for coverage. *Disclosure: Tom works at Huntress, whose telemetry is cited in this story. Nothing here rests on it, and the analysis this story draws on is independent.*

- **ARTEX** is an open-source, LLM-driven agentic penetration testing framework written in Go. A planner agent takes an objective and dispatches worker agents around Bash, Nmap and curl. It was published in mid 2026 under AGPL-3.0 and the developer took the repository down on October 8 after it turned up in attacks on South Korean banks. Forks, localised copies and a paid build circulate.
- The FBI says a variety of actors have weaponized it since at least August 2026. Microsoft attributes **Storm-0940** to more than **2,250 Microsoft 365 tenants** using stolen ConnectWise Cloud Backup application credentials, and tracks **Storm-3358**, a Cambodia-based financially motivated group, from October 2026. Keep the word reach: those are attempts against tenants, not 2,250 confirmed compromises.
- Censys and Check Point both counted publicly exposed consoles, more than 1,600 in one count and 800 plus in another. Cite the number you are using and who produced it, because the two scanners do not agree.

### Reality check: fast, not new

ARTEX has not reinvented offensive security. It runs techniques that have worked for twenty years, just faster.

- Nmap scanning since 1997. The OWASP web classes since the early 2000s. Password spraying, secrets in public repositories, and Kerberoasting since 2014. OAuth and service principal abuse from Solorigate in 2020 through Midnight Blizzard in 2024. Bulk Key Vault and storage key reads as old as the cloud itself.
- What changed is tempo and economics. Sub three hours to the objective in the reported cases. 2,900 failed sign-ins a minute at peak in one campaign. A campaign costed at roughly 145 to 1,500 dollars, mostly compute. An agent that retried a failed ARM call with a different API version and succeeded in 28 to 80 seconds.
- Most of the successful paths used credentials that were already valid. No phish, no MFA prompt. IP blocklists and static indicators age in minutes, so hunt behaviour instead: 27 cloud permission probes in 23 seconds, incrementing strings in object names, `artex-probe-*` files, `ArtexProbe` accounts. Do not key on user agents, because the same behaviour comes from CyberStrikeAI, HexStrike and PentAGI.
- Microsoft's answer is correlation across Defender XDR plus attack disruption. That answer depends on licensing and on the signals being switched on, which is not the same as having the tool.

**CIS Controls:** 5.4 and 6.5 (Account Management, and Access Control Management), 6.8 (role-based access), 8.11 (Audit Log Management), 12.4 (Network Infrastructure Management), 15.1 (Service Provider Management). **NIS2:** Article 21(2)(d) on supply chain security.

## ConnectWise ScreenConnect: the RMM as the attack path

**Sourcing:** ConnectWise's Trust Center advisory, Huntress incident telemetry and the published indicators, BleepingComputer, and Sophos's independent sightings. *Disclosure: Tom works at Huntress, which found this campaign and published the victim counts cited here.*

- From about 17:47 UTC on November 11 an attacker with access to partner ScreenConnect instances used the built-in Run Command to push a second, attacker-controlled ScreenConnect client to endpoints. Huntress counted **6 compromised MSPs, 286 plus endpoints and 47 downstream organizations**, still rising at publication.
- **The reverse pivot is the story.** The modified client impersonates the legitimate one, so when a technician opens the panel to investigate, the infected endpoint recruits them. The kit drops `sqlite3.exe` to read saved browser sessions and authentication tokens, then pivots to the panels that technician can reach. One compromised MSP becomes the next one's initial access.
- ConnectWise reports no evidence of compromise of its own infrastructure and no ScreenConnect vulnerability. Its initial assessment, which it says is still being validated, points at five third-party extensions in the RMM+ family that expose a remote-command API behind a partner-set passphrase. Roughly 400 partner environments have one installed.
- The September rogue-client flaw, **CVE-2026-84869**, is a client-side file-transfer and access issue rated 9.9 and in CISA's KEV catalog since September 11. Its remediation required reinstalling host clients and access agents, not only patching the server. Nobody has linked it to this campaign, and Blackpoint says no new vulnerability was seen. A patched panel does not close that path on its own.

### Detection note

The kit has a cleanup stage. It removes the scheduled task, the Run key, the working folder and the `sckit-*` files, and restarts the services it stopped. Live-state inspection of a hit host can come back clean. Go to the logs: System 7045 for new ScreenConnect client service installs, Security 4698 for the scheduled task, and the Run Command history, including the older entries.

**CIS Controls:** 2.1 and 2.5 (Inventory and Control of Software Assets, including every extension), 4.1 and 4.8 (Secure Configuration), 5.4 (Account Management), 6.4 (Access Control Management), 8.11 (Audit Log Management), 13.11 (Network Monitoring and Defense).

## wfx-loot: the CI pipeline as a credential store

**Sourcing:** Aikido's write-up and SafeDep's independent analysis of the same campaign, plus SecurityWeek and Help Net Security. Both vendor analyses are telemetry from their own sensors. Neither establishes how the 4,837 accounts were compromised in the first place.

- From about 12:45 UTC on November 10, **4,837 compromised accounts** opened probe pull requests from a `wfx-docs` branch to **61,764 repositories**, 10,431 of which the account could write to as a collaborator or organization member. The payload arrived as a workflow edit, an npm postinstall hook that runs on a developer laptop, or a new workflow in an empty repository.
- Keep the three counts apart. 61,764 repositories received a pull request. 8,093 received malicious commits. 1,806 to 1,835 actually ran the payload. This is not a supply chain attack in the npm sense, the two tainted packages are a side effect of release triggers.
- The payload reads repository secrets, `GITHUB_TOKEN`, the OIDC token, kubeconfigs and cloud credentials. It then persists three ways: forging the required-check context names so a malicious commit appears to pass CI, rewriting a chained AWS role trust policy to `Principal: *`, and adding Entra federated credentials named `wfx-ci`.
- It cleans up within about a minute. A gap in the workflow run numbering is often the only trace left, so a clean audit is not a clean bill.

**CIS Controls:** 4.1 and 4.8 (Secure Configuration), 5.4 (Account Management), 6.4 and 6.5 (Access Control Management), 8.11 (Audit Log Management), 12.4 and 12.6 (Network Infrastructure Management), 13.11 (Network Monitoring and Defense), 15.4 (Service Provider Management), 16.5 (Application Software Security).

## Cursor: the credential in the training data

**Sourcing:** Cursor's investigation update and marketplace security notice, Microsoft Threat Intelligence's independent analysis of the payload, BleepingComputer and SecurityWeek. This closes the open loop carried from [EP7](/ep/7/).

- The chain starts at a vendor, not at Cursor. An access key Cursor issued to its training and inference provider was compromised there, and the attacker used it to read a bucket of coding agent traces, mostly from 2025. A small number of traces contained unredacted credentials, because customers had pasted them in.
- On October 29 a credential taken from that data placed malicious copies of the Claude Code extension on the cache that serves Cursor's marketplace. The window was 2:27am to 1:35am, about 11 hours, and up to 5,000 users may have installed it.
- The payload wrote `svc-telemetry` into `authorized.json`, attempted a persistent SSH backdoor, and dropped a scheduled task or LaunchAgent. The SSH attempt mostly failed, but the artifacts landed.
- Keep the three population numbers apart: up to 5,000 users exposed to the extension, a small number of customers whose traces were read, and a smaller number whose credentials were in those traces. Cursor's own wording is the only defensible version.
- Anthropic's involvement is as the publisher whose name was used. The extension was not built by Anthropic and this is not an Anthropic vulnerability. The root cause sits in Cursor's vendor chain and in the traces that were stored.

**CIS Controls:** 2.1 (Inventory and Control of Software Assets), 3.1 and 3.2 (Data Protection), 4.1 (Secure Configuration), 6.8 (role-based access), 15.1, 15.3 and 15.4 (Service Provider Management).

## Infoblox: what a DNS snapshot is worth

**Sourcing:** Infoblox's statement to press, TechCrunch, The Register and Security Affairs. The detail on what was taken comes from The Register's sources and a CEO email the outlet says it saw, not from a published advisory. Infoblox is private, so there is no filing.

- Infoblox confirmed on November 16 that it is investigating unauthorized access to company information through a compromised third-party data migration vendor. The vendor did SharePoint migration work and held delegated access. The Register's sources say the attacker used that vendor's accounts to exfiltrate from SharePoint.
- Reported taken includes configuration information for some customers' NIOS deployments, meaning saved snapshots of DNS and DHCP configuration, Grid settings and reporting logs. A DNS and DHCP snapshot is a map of the network even when it contains no credentials.
- Infoblox says it contained the incident and that its products, cloud services, updates and build pipeline are not affected. That list is narrower than "the company is fine". No group has claimed it, and the company has not said how many customers or staff are affected.
- The disclosure shape is the story. No advisory of its own, no filing, no notification detail. Ask your vendor in writing whether your backups were in scope, and put the answer in the ticket.

**CIS Controls:** 3.1, 3.2 and 3.12 (Data Protection), 4.1 (Secure Configuration), 6.4 and 6.8 (Access Control Management), 8.11 (Audit Log Management), 12.4 (Network Infrastructure Management), 15.1, 15.3 and 15.4 (Service Provider Management). **NIS2:** Article 21(2)(d) and Article 28 on supply chain and service provider obligations.

## Microsoft 365: the fix that broke five months later

**Sourcing:** Microsoft's preliminary post-incident report for incidents EX1550543 and MO1550821, quoted second-hand where the admin center is gated, plus The Register, mainstream coverage and aggregator timelines. No independent technical analysis of the defect exists. The final report is due November 20.

- The impact window was November 12 at 16:20 UTC to November 13 at 16:10 UTC, just under 24 hours. Three factors combined. A latent defect in a fix built specifically for the Microsoft 365 networking gateway servers and deployed in July caused lock contention. Teams had failed over to the primary gateway about 15 hours earlier, adding load. And nearly 14 percent of gateway capacity was already out from an unrelated issue the day before.
- Rollback was not possible because the defect was months old. Engineers wrote an update that returned the code path to the known good state. Exchange Online could not fail out of the gateway, which is why mail was the visible failure while Teams recovered.
- The bulkheads did not hold. Automatic rerouting spread the load, and within two hours a large share of gateway capacity was degraded. The numbers to quote are Microsoft's, not the headlines' inflated versions.
- The lesson is not a Microsoft lesson. It is a tabletop lesson. A five month old defect in a fix is a change management failure, and "we would fail over" is a sentence that needs testing against the failure mode you actually have.

**CIS Controls:** 4.1 and 4.8 (Secure Configuration), 7.4 (Continuous Vulnerability Management), 16.7 (Application Software Security), 17.4 (Incident Response Management).

## The FAA ground stop: two routers and a default feature

**Sourcing:** The Saturday FAA press conference with technology chief Rebecca Guy on record, the contractor's public statement, Tom's Hardware, WSJ, CNBC and TechCrunch. Root cause is not established and the FAA says so.

- Friday November 13, communications failed at the Indianapolis Center, which covers northern Kentucky, southern Indiana and surrounding airspace. The FAA traced it to a radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas and a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, both recently fitted with Juniper routers by L3Harris. The sites were pinging at roughly a thousand times every four to five seconds.
- 17 of the 22 air route traffic control centers lost communications and the FAA issued a nationwide ground stop. Disabling the two routers stabilized the network, and the FAA then changed a router feature across its roughly 60 Juniper routers. More than 8,200 flights were canceled Friday and more were canceled Saturday.
- Two conflations to avoid. The FAA's modernization program is not the cause, and the aged copper is not the cause. The routers were new and the feature was enabled by default. HPE says the devices worked as intended, which is the more uncomfortable statement, not the defense it sounds like.
- Third nationwide ground stop of the century. The engineering question is why two new edge routers could flood a network shared by 22 centers with no isolation in front of them.

**CIS Controls:** 4.1 and 4.8 (Secure Configuration), 12.4 and 12.6 (Network Infrastructure Management), 17.4 (Incident Response Management). Change validation on network infrastructure belongs in the change process, not in the vendor's default configuration.

## What to do before Monday

1. **Inventory the RMM extensions and the credentials behind them** (CIS 2.1, 2.5, 6.4). If a ScreenConnect extension exposes a remote-command API behind a shared passphrase, that passphrase is an administrative credential. Rotate it, and read Run Command history back past the incident window.
2. **Rotate CI secrets at the source, not the symptom** (CIS 5.4, 15.4). Anything reachable from a GitHub Actions workflow that ran a pull request from an unknown branch in the last two weeks. Check for Entra federated credentials named `wfx-ci` and AWS role trust policies widened to `Principal: *`.
3. **Ask your DNS vendor in writing** (CIS 15.3, 15.4). Whether your configuration backups were in scope of a third-party migration or support engagement. The answer belongs in the ticket.
4. **Put the M365 outage in your tabletop** (CIS 17.4). A five month old defect with no rollback path, a failed failover, and a 24 hour window. If your mail flow depends on one vendor gateway, say out loud what you do for the first six hours.
